About the artist

Paul Sergounine

Paul Sergounine (b. 1984) is a contemporary painter working between Tbilisi and Düsseldorf. He is represented by Galerie Bruno Massa.

He grew up in Düsseldorf, where he graduated from the International School of Düsseldorf in 2003. He studied at Webster University in Leiden and at ISG in Paris before completing a BA in International Management at Webster University Vienna in 2008. After many years living and working internationally, he established his studio in Tbilisi in 2021.

His ongoing series, Mind Mechanics (2025–), is conceived as a cycle of 250 paintings in five fixed formats, from 100 × 120 to 200 × 240 cm, numbered in the order made rather than titled. It began as an investigation into memory, perception, identity and the internal structures of consciousness. A recurring head profile runs throughout — not a portrait but an architectural framework, a vessel for thought rather than identity.

The early works established a distinctive visual language through oil stick and oil pastel on black acrylic grounds. As the series developed, Sergounine increasingly expanded his materials and working methods, introducing successive layers, physical excavation and the cannibalisation of former works. In later paintings, spray paint and parchment paper join the oil stick and oil pastel; layers are built up, concealed and scraped back, so that pigments from previously separated stages mix and exposed fragments of earlier compositions stand against newly introduced layers. Cannibalisation, which began with No. 56, means that earlier paintings are cut apart and selected fragments are incorporated into new compositions. The psychological origins and recurring forms of the series remain. A parallel series, Totems (2025–), develops the same concerns in vertical, architectural form.

In February 2027 his work will be shown at the CICA Museum in Gimpo-si, South Korea. In April 2027, Sergounine is scheduled to participate in an artist residency at the Ria Keburia Foundation in Georgia.

Statement

I do not paint to decorate, illustrate, or comfort. I paint to expose the unstable architecture of thought.

Mind Mechanics began as an investigation into the invisible systems through which memory, perception, identity and instinct are constructed. Rather than depicting external reality, I set out to examine the internal mechanics of consciousness: how experience accumulates, fragments, erodes and reorganises over time. The recurring forms of the series, above all the head profile, grew out of that question, and they remain at the centre of the work.

Over time, the paintings have become increasingly physical. The psychological investigation has not been abandoned; it has moved into the material. What began as a question about how the mind builds and revises its own structures is now worked out in the substance of the painting itself, in acrylic ground, oil stick, oil pastel, spray paint and parchment paper.

I build each painting through successive cycles of construction, accumulation and concealment. Layers bury earlier compositions, and then I dig back into them. I scrape not merely to destroy, but to discover what lies beneath. As I excavate, pigments from separate stages of the painting are exposed and physically mixed, and fragments of earlier compositions surface against the layers that were meant to cover them. The tension between these states, between what the painting was and what it is becoming, is where the work takes place. The finished surface is a physical record of its own transformation.

Beginning with Mind Mechanics No. 56, I introduced a process I call the cannibalisation of former works. Earlier paintings that no longer satisfy me are cut apart, and selected fragments become part of the material foundation of a new painting. Cannibalisation takes excavation beyond the single canvas: material from works that once stood on their own enters new compositions, and their visual and physical histories meet. Destruction becomes a means of continuation. The former painting loses its autonomy, while its material and visual history survives within another work.

I have no interest in the finish that training produces. The mark stays as it was made — hurried, corrected, sometimes wrong — because refinement would erase precisely what the painting is a record of. Spontaneity here is not an absence of rigour but its condition. I intervene deliberately, yet I cannot fully predict what the materials will do once layers are opened and pigments meet. The structure only means something if something inside it is allowed to go wrong.

My paintings do not seek definitive answers. Each one is less an image of the mind than an active model of how it is continually formed, buried, uncovered and transformed.
